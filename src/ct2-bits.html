Negative numbers and field widths

Use a fixed number of bits to write −5 and recognise a number that will not fit.

Lesson 5 of 22 / Lectures 2–3

See what eight 1s can mean

11111111 means 255 when we read it as unsigned, so every position adds its positive place value. If the same eight bits are read as signed two’s complement, the leftmost place is −128 and the other places add up to 127. The result is −1. The bits stay the same. The instruction or the question tells you how to read them.

Write a negative number

Start with +5 in eight positions. Flip every bit, then add 1 in binary. Any extra carry outside the eight positions is discarded. The last line is how −5 fits into eight bits.

StepEight bits
Write +500000101
Flip bits11111010
Add 111111011

To make a signed value wider while preserving it, copy its leftmost bit into the new positions. This is sign extension. So −5 becomes 11111111 11111011 in 16 bits. To extend an unsigned value, add zeros on the left. That is zero extension.

Count what can fit

Each bit doubles the possible patterns. Eight bits give 28 = 256 patterns. Unsigned uses 0 through 255. Signed uses −128 through 127. For n bits, unsigned is 0 through 2n − 1, signed is −2n−1 through 2n−1 − 1. addi uses a signed 16-bit immediate, from −32,768 to 32,767, and sign-extends it to the 32-bit register width. Overflow means an arithmetic result falls outside its available range.

Shifts and bitwise operations

Watch bits slide, then compare them one column at a time.

Lesson 6 of 22 / Lectures 2–3

What does sll mean?

sll stands for shift left logical. In sll $t0, $t1, 2, $t0 is where the result goes, $t1 supplies the bits, and 2 is how many places to shift. Each move fills the new rightmost place with 0 and discards whichever bit leaves the left edge. “Logical” means the new places are filled with zeros. The small display below shows eight of the register’s 32 positions.

For small numbers that fit, one left shift doubles the number. Two make it four times as large. srl stands for shift right logical. It fills from the left with 0 and drops bits on the right. For an unsigned value, one right shift divides by two and drops the remainder. So 5 becomes 2. For signed negative numbers, logical right shift does a different job from signed division.

    Apply one rule to each pair of bits

    Suppose $t1 holds 5 and $t2 holds 3. Line up their binary digits. Bitwise means you look at one column at a time and write one result bit in that same column. In the 4s column, the inputs are 1 and 0. AND writes 0 because it needs two 1s. OR writes 1 because it needs at least one 1.

    and $t0, $t1, $t2 writes 1 into $t0. or $t0, $t1, $t2 writes 7 because the 4s, 2s, and 1s columns are on. Both source registers keep their values.

    NOT flips each bit. NOR means do OR, then flip its result. The OR result here is 7, with 29 zeros followed by three 1s. Flipping all 32 bits leaves 29 ones followed by three zeros. That pattern represents −8 as a signed 32-bit integer. 1000 in the table shows just the last four bits of that pattern.

    Use a number written in the instruction

    Let $t1 still hold 5. In andi $t0, $t1, 3, the CPU reads 5 from $t1, takes 3 straight from the instruction, and writes the AND result to $t0. The 3 is the immediate. The letter i in andi and ori tells you that the last input is a number written in the instruction.

           $t1 = 5     0101
   immediate 3     0011
                   ────
  andi result      0001  = 1
   ori result      0111  = 7

    The 3 in andi acts as a mask, a pattern that picks positions to keep. At each column where the mask has a 1, AND copies the input bit. Where the mask has a 0, AND writes 0. So 0011 keeps the rightmost two bits of 0101, which are 01. With ori $t0, $t1, 3, a 1 in the constant instead sets that result bit to 1. The 2s bit turns on, and the result is 7.

    Translate ~(a | 5) in two instructions

    In C, | means bitwise OR, & means bitwise AND, and ~ means bitwise NOT. Parentheses tell you to do what’s inside first. Suppose a = 3, stored in $t0. Put the answer in $t1.

    1. ori $t1, $t0, 5 does the part inside the parentheses. 0011 OR 0101 gives 0111, so $t1 now holds 7.
    2. nor $t1, $t1, $zero reads that 7 and writes back to the same register. OR with zero leaves every bit as it was, so the NOR flips all 32 bits. $t1 now holds −8. $t0 still holds 3.
    Worked variation · What if the expression is ~(a & n)?

    Suppose a = 5 in $t0 and n = 3 in $t1. Both inputs are already in registers, so use and $t2, $t0, $t1. That puts 1 in $t2. Then nor $t2, $t2, $zero flips the 32-bit pattern for 1, giving 0xFFFFFFFE, or −2 signed.
