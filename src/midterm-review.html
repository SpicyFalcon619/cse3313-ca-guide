Lecture 4 · Midterm review

Performance in four steps

Start with how long one task takes. Then learn to calculate that time and predict which changes will shorten it.

Work through these in order
1

Compare the time for the same task

Suppose your program finishes in 10 seconds on A and 15 seconds on B. A is faster by a factor of 15 ÷ 10 = 1.5×. Use old time ÷ new time for speedup.

Response time measures how long one task takes. Throughput measures how many tasks finish per unit of time. An extra core may finish more independent tasks each hour while the time for one single-threaded task stays the same.

Elapsed time is what a stopwatch measures from start to finish. CPU time is the time the CPU spends working on the task, including operating-system CPU work on its behalf. For a simple single-threaded example, 5 s of user CPU work, 1 s of system CPU work and 4 s of waiting gives 6 s of CPU time and 10 s elapsed.

Read the full lessons on performance and elapsed and CPU time.

2

Calculate CPU time

A clock cycle is one tick of the CPU’s clock. At 2.5 GHz, there are 2.5 billion ticks per second, or 2.5 ticks per nanosecond. If a program uses 10 cycles, it takes 10 ÷ 2.5 = 4 ns at that clock rate.

Instruction count is the number of instructions that actually ran. CPI is the average number of cycles per instruction. Multiply instruction count by CPI to get cycles, then divide by clock rate to get CPU time. When each instruction type has its own CPI, multiply each count by its CPI and add those results. Divide total cycles by total instructions only if asked for average CPI.

Worked question · Which of three processors is fastest?

Assume the same instruction count on all three processors. Comparing CPU time per instruction is enough. Divide CPI by the rate in GHz to get ns per instruction.

ProcessorRateCPITime per instruction
P13 GHz1.50.50 ns
P22.5 GHz1.00.40 ns
P34 GHz2.20.55 ns

P2 is fastest. It is 0.50 ÷ 0.40 = 1.25× as fast as P1. Sharing an instruction set only means the processors understand the same instructions. Check that they run the same number of instructions before using this shortcut.

If the question gives 10 seconds of CPU time instead, first multiply time by rate. P1 runs 10 × 3 billion = 30 billion cycles, and 30 billion ÷ 1.5 = 20 billion instructions.

Practise clock calculations, weighted CPI and the CPU time equation.

3

Compare power before and after a change

Voltage supplies the electrical push. A 15% drop leaves 85% of the original voltage. In the lecture’s dynamic power model, voltage is squared, so if only voltage changes, new power ÷ old power = 0.85² = 0.7225. That is 72.25% of the old power, or a 27.75% drop.

Dynamic power is the power used to switch the circuits. The model multiplies effective capacitance (how much charge switches at a given voltage), voltage squared and clock frequency. To compare two settings, multiply the new-to-old ratio for each factor, squaring the voltage ratio. If all three factors drop by 15%, the power ratio is 0.85⁴ ≈ 0.522.

The power wall explains how power and heat constrain clock speed.

4

Keep the part of a program that stays the same

A 280 ns program uses 80 ns for floating-point (FP) arithmetic, 180 ns for loads and stores, and 20 ns for branches. If FP runs 20× as fast, only the 80 ns changes. It becomes 80 ÷ 20 = 4 ns. Add the fixed 200 ns to get 204 ns total, so you save 76 ns.

That is Amdahl’s law in time units. Split the old time into affected and fixed parts. Divide the affected time by its speedup and add the fixed time.

When a question says “reduced by 20”

Trimester 233 Q1(b)(i) gives “20” without specifying the unit in the question. State what you are assuming and show the line where you use it.

Worked questions · Can the whole program reach a target speed?

For a 2× overall speedup on the 280 ns program, the target is 140 ns. Improving only loads and stores leaves 80 + 20 = 100 ns fixed. That gives loads and stores 140 − 100 = 40 ns, down from 180 ns. They need a 4.5× speedup.

In a 160 ns program with 100 ns affected and 60 ns fixed, a 3× overall speedup would require about 53.33 ns. Even making the affected part take zero time would leave 60 ns. The theoretical ceiling is 160 ÷ 60 ≈ 2.67×.

Try more problems in Amdahl’s law and past midterms.

Check yourself
How long do 12 cycles take at 3 GHz?

12 ÷ 3 = 4 ns. At 3 GHz the clock ticks three times per nanosecond.

What if the affected 30 ns of a 100 ns program becomes three times as fast?

The fixed part is 70 ns. The affected part becomes 10 ns. The new total is 80 ns and the overall speedup is 100 ÷ 80 = 1.25×.

Lectures 5–7 · Midterm review

Read, trace and encode MIPS

Start by following one value. Then follow where the processor goes next, and finish by turning a whole instruction into bits.

Work through these in order
1

Do the arithmetic in registers

A register holds a number inside the CPU. If $s0 holds x and $s1 holds y, you can calculate z = x*8 + y/2 - 7 in $s2 with the following instructions, taking y to be nonnegative and the signed intermediate values and result to fit.

sll  $t0, $s0, 3
srl  $t1, $s1, 1
add  $s2, $t0, $t1
addi $s2, $s2, -7

Moving a bit one place left doubles its value, so a left shift by 3 gives a factor of 8. A logical right shift by 1 divides an unsigned or nonnegative value by 2 and discards the remainder. To get the unsigned remainder after division by 4, use andi with 3 to keep the bottom two bits. For x*5, compute x*4 and add x.

2

Find a word before you read or write it

If a four-byte integer array starts at byte 100, A[3] is three elements after the start. Its byte offset is 3 × 4 = 12, so its address is 112. lw reads one word from that address into a register. sw writes one word from a register there.

Worked example · Turn A[10] = h + B[i] into MIPS

Set $s0 to A’s base address, $s1 to B’s base, $s2 to h (230 in the lecture) and $s3 to i. Use a register to hold the address of B[i]. The offset written inside lw has to be a number, so use 0 when the base register already holds the full address.

sll $t1, $s3, 2
add $t1, $s1, $t1
lw  $t0, 0($t1)
add $t2, $s2, $t0
sw  $t2, 40($s0)

For a constant index you can write the byte offset directly. For A[12] = h + A[8], load at offset 32, add h to the loaded value and store at offset 48.

Try this with the word memory visualiser. To practise, translate k = B[3] + 5 by loading at offset 12 and adding 5 to the loaded value.

3

Trace the next instruction

A label names a place in the program. beq jumps there when two registers are equal. bne jumps there when they differ. slt and slti put 1 in a register when the first value is less than the second, and 0 otherwise.

Worked example · Check i <= 10 at the end of a loop

For integer i, i <= 10 means i < 11. With i in $s0, write these two lines after the loop body.

slti $t0, $s0, 11
bne  $t0, $zero, Loop

A do loop starts with the body and checks at the end. A while or for checks before each body. In a for loop, initialise once, then check, run the body, update and go back to the check. If a while condition compares save[5] with k and the body only changes i, the body leaves the comparison unchanged.

For i < j && i != k, test the first condition and branch past the body when it fails. Then test the second and do the same. Keep the register map nearby so an update to j changes the register for j.

Trace more branches and loops.

4

Follow a function call and its stack space

Before calling add(5, 10), put the two arguments in $a0 and $a1. jal saves the resume address in $ra and jumps to the function. The function puts 15 in $v0 and jr $ra resumes the caller. Put helper code after an exit or explicit jump so that normal execution skips it after resuming.

Worked example · Four saved registers

$sp holds the stack pointer. Reserve four words by subtracting 16 from it. Save the four registers your function needs to preserve at offsets 12, 8, 4 and 0 from the new $sp. Before returning, reload each register from its own address and add 16 back to $sp. If this function calls another function and then returns, preserve its incoming $ra across that call, since jal writes a new one.

In the notebook’s nested example, x = 15 and y = 7 give m = x+y+10 = 32 and n = m-2*x = 2. Then f(15, 7, 32, 2) returns 50+(15+7)-(32+2) = 38. The caller subtracts y and gets 31.

Watch a call happen in the function and stack visualiser.

5

Write the fields for one instruction

A field is a group of bits in an instruction. For lw $s1, 200($s2), write the I-format fields in this order. The opcode is 35, the base register $s2 is 18, the destination $s1 is 17 and the offset is 200 bytes. Encode those values in 6, 5, 5 and 16 bits respectively.

Worked examples · Arithmetic, a branch and a jump

R-format uses opcode / rs / rt / rd / shamt / funct at widths 6/5/5/5/5/6. For add $s1, $s2, $t1, those values are 0 / 18 / 9 / 17 / 0 / 32. For srl $s1, $s2, 20, they are 0 / 0 / 18 / 17 / 20 / 2. For jr $ra, they are 0 / 31 / 0 / 0 / 0 / 8.

I-format uses opcode / rs / rt / immediate. For andi $s1, $s2, 223, write 12 / 18 / 17 / 223. andi treats that 16-bit field as unsigned.

For bne $s1, $s2, target at PC 1000 with a target at byte 1104, the register fields are rs = 17 and rt = 18. The displacement from the next instruction at 1004 is 100 bytes, so the immediate is 100 ÷ 4 = 25. Start with target − (PC + 4) to find the displacement.

J-format has a 6-bit opcode and a 26-bit target field. For j 2000 in the low address region, the target field is 2000 ÷ 4 = 500. The processor supplies the upper four address bits from PC + 4. Five bits can name 32 registers, so a design with 64 registers needs six bits per register field.

Check the bits with the instruction encoder and review branch and jump fields.

Check yourself
What is the byte offset for A[5] in a four-byte array?

5 × 4 = 20 bytes.

Which register holds the address that jr $ra uses?

$ra. After a call made with jal, it holds the address where the caller resumes in the course’s simplified model.

Arithmetic · Midterm review

Unsigned multiplication by shifts and adds

Multiply 6 by 7 using four-bit inputs. Follow where the bits go at each step.

Build the partial products before using the combined register

Start with the multiplication you already know, 6 × 7. The multiplier is 0111, so its bits stand for 0 copies of 8, 1 copy of 4, 1 copy of 2, and 1 copy of 1. Multiply 6 by the places that are on. The separate results are called partial products.

PlaceBit in 0111ContributionRunning total
116 × 1 = 66
216 × 2 = 1218
416 × 4 = 2442
80Skip 6 × 8.42

A left shift by one place doubles each partial product. The combined-register method below gets the same answer while shifting the accumulating product right each time. Use four passes for a four-bit multiplier, including passes whose current bit is zero.

Open the simulator lab and choose Unsigned shift-and-add multiplication under Exam practice. Set 14 and 10 to compare a different pattern with the example.

Work out 14 × 10 using only the 1s in binary 1010

The 1s are at places 2 and 8. The partial products are 14 × 2 = 28 and 14 × 8 = 112. Add them to get 140, or 10001100. It needs eight bits even though both inputs fit in four.

Worked example

Use one eight-bit register split into an upper half and a lower half. Start with 0 in the upper half and 7 (0111) in the lower half. Keep 6 (0110) in a separate register. The space between the two groups of bits marks the split.

Look at the rightmost bit. If it is 1, add 6 to the upper half. Then shift both halves right together by one bit, carrying any overflow from the upper-half addition into the shift. Repeat once for each of the four multiplier bits.

StepStarting valueUpper-half workAfter the shift
10000 0111Rightmost bit 1. Upper becomes 0110.0011 0011
20011 0011Rightmost bit 1. Upper becomes 1001.0100 1001
30100 1001Rightmost bit 1. Upper becomes 1010.0101 0100
40101 0100Rightmost bit 0. Upper stays 0101 before the shift.0010 1010

The final register holds 0010 1010, which is 32 + 8 + 2 = 42. In step 2 the low bit of 1001 moves across the split and becomes the first bit of the lower half.

Check yourself
If the rightmost bit is zero, what do you do in that step?

Skip the addition and still shift the combined register right by one bit.
